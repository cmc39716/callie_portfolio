01 / OVERVIEW

Recognizable by Design.

The Georgia Museum of Natural History combines research, education, and public outreach through collecting and documenting the natural and cultural history of Georgia and beyond. This class project translates the museum's artifacts into a visual system built around recognizable animals, creating interest before visitors even arrive at the museum.

The challenge was to develop a cohesive set of pictograms that could become the foundation for an advertising poster series. Each symbol needed to remain recognizable at both large and small scales while preserving the distinctive characteristics of its animal.

CONCEPUTAL CLIENT
02 / Research

Collections Are Key.

My research began by exploring the identity and purpose of the Georgia Museum of Natural History through its online records and in-person exhibits.

The museum contains 14 natural-history collections that support research, teaching, public service, and community outreach. Its mission to preserve Georgia's natural and cultural heritage became a central influence on the design direction.

The pictograms needed to feel connected to the regional environment while remaining accessible to audiences beyond the scientific community.

03 / Exploration

Finding the Right Forms.

I began studying animals that could represent the museum's collections and the natural landscape of the surrounding region. I selected a fox, bear, and ibis. Their different proportions allowed each animal to stand apart, while shared construction methods and similar stances helped create a cohesive visual language.

04 / Development

Detail to Reduction.

My initial sketches included detailed claws, facial features, wing structures, and fur textures. I then filled the drawings entirely with black to evaluate whether their silhouettes remained recognizable without internal detail.

Through repeated simplification, I removed unnecessary features while preserving the visual elements that made each animal distinct. This shifted the work from illustration toward pictogram design.

Bold silhouettes, curved forms, and negative space became the foundation of the visual system.

05 / Visual Language

One System.

Each pictogram uses a shared visual language while communicating the individual characteristics of each animal. The fox expresses agility through its upright posture and sweeping tail. The ibis is defined by its long legs, curved neck, and pointed beak. The bear uses heavier, grounded forms to communicate mass and strength.

Successful pictograms are not about drawing an animal with fewer details. They are about discovering which details tell the story.

06 / Poster Concept

Eye on Conservation.

The next challenge was translating the pictograms into a poster series centered around the theme "Eye on Conservation."

Observation is essential to natural-history research. Specimens are collected, examined, documented, and studied to develop a deeper understanding of the world. I wanted the posters to communicate that same sense of discovery.

Typography became an active element of the composition rather than simply supporting the illustrations.

07 / Composition

Discovering Balance.

I experimented with hierarchy through oversized pictograms, smaller animal figures, overlapping typography, circular text, and large areas of negative space.

These explorations helped establish the relationship between the illustration and the message, allowing the typography to interact directly with each animal.

Built for
Discovery.

Three distinct animals. One cohesive visual system.

08 / Outcome

A Cohesive Visual Identity.

The final posters combine typography, texture, and color into a unified visual system. Each composition follows the same underlying design principles while highlighting the distinctive characteristics of the fox, ibis, and bear.

Together, the posters communicate the Georgia Museum of Natural History's focus on research and community, inviting viewers to discover more about the natural world around them.

09 / Refinement

Refining the Details.

Throughout the project, weekly critiques, peer reviews, and test prints helped refine the hierarchy, legibility, composition, and consistency of the work.

I also gathered feedback from people working in science to evaluate how effectively the posters communicated for an institution centered on research and education.

Printing the posters at full scale was particularly important because it allowed me to evaluate the relationships between typography, texture, and image at their intended viewing size.

10 / Reflection

The Art of Simplification.

This project strengthened my understanding of how to simplify complex visual subjects without losing the characteristics that make them recognizable.

It also taught me to prioritize broad compositional decisions before refining smaller details, while considering multiple viewing distances and the existing identity of an organization.

Ultimately, I developed a cohesive visual language that made research, collections, and natural history more approachable for the audiences the museum hopes to reach.
